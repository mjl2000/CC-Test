Trans Mountain Pipeline (TMX): Route & Broader First Nations Territories

Edmonton, AB → Westridge Marine Terminal, Burnaby, BC · plus the proposed 2026 Bruderheim–Roberts Bank expansion · basemap from real coastline/border/river data, territories schematic

Read this before the map. The coastline, provincial border, rivers, and lakes below are real simplified geometry (Natural Earth 1:50m data), not hand-drawn approximations. The territory areas are still schematic: polygons anchored to landmarks each Nation itself cites (e.g. Howe Sound, Mt Garibaldi, the Athabasca headwaters, the Arrow Lakes), not surveyed or legally-binding boundaries. Many Nations' territories extend further still; where a Nation's own site gives a precise area figure, its polygon roughly targets that scale. Overlap between areas reflects genuinely overlapping/shared or contested claims reported in the sources below, not map error. This is an educational reference assembled from Nations' own public statements, court records, Canada Energy Regulator filings, and news reporting; it is not a legal document and should not be used for consultation, land-claim, or governmental decision-making. Where a boundary leans on general/crowdsourced-style reference rather than a primary document, that is noted in each entry. Methodology

Coastline, the Alberta–BC border, major rivers (Fraser, Athabasca, North Saskatchewan, Columbia) and select lakes are real, simplified boundary/hydrography data from Natural Earth (1:50m cultural & physical vectors), clipped and lightly decimated for this map, not hand-approximated. Pipeline waypoints are drawn from Trans Mountain's 2013 CER/NEB Project Description, CER pipeline-profile and watercourse-crossing records, and corroborating news/encyclopedic sources. Territory polygons are schematic areas anchored to specific landmarks each Nation's own materials, BC Environmental Assessment Office consultation appendices, CER filings, or court records associate with their territory (e.g. Squamish's own site names Howe Sound, Whistler, and Burrard Inlet); landmark coordinates were independently geocoded, not estimated by eye. The full-territory shapes with a cited area figure (SSN ~12,500 km², Squamish ~6,732 km², Tsleil-Waututh ~1,865 km², Musqueam ~5,330 km²) are uniformly scaled about their centroid to match that figure, then rendered as a smoothed curve through the landmark vertices rather than a hard-edged polygon, so the shape reads as an approximate zone rather than a surveyed boundary; the five corridor bands (Enoch Cree Nation, Aseniwuche Winewak, Simpcw, Coldwater Indian Band, Stó:lō) are drawn at a fixed width along the relevant valley/corridor and are not area-matched, since they represent a travel corridor rather than a full territorial outline. Rather than one blended "Secwépemc territory" shape, this map breaks the Secwépemc-related claims apart by source: Simpcw's own North Thompson valley territory (its own cited hectare figure); Stk'emlupsemc te Secwépemc Nation's (SSN's) own 2015, ~1.25-million-hectare title claim centred on Kamloops (filled); and the separate, broader Dec. 2003 BC Supreme Court joint title claim by Adams Lake, Bonaparte, High Bar, Neskonlith, Simpcw, Spallumcheen, and Whispering Pines (dashed outline, since its landmark description is coarser and was not independently verified beyond four named places). These three overlap in places by design; that is what the source record actually shows; it is not one Secwépemc Nation boundary drawn three times. The Stó:lō zone stays a single blended shape deliberately: unlike the other groupings on this map, the Stó:lō Collective genuinely did negotiate TMX consultation as one body, so blending them is the accurate choice, not a simplification. The former "Treaty 6 Nations" and "Nlaka'pamux / Scw'exmx Tribal Council" zones were each narrowed to a single flagship band (Enoch Cree Nation; Coldwater Indian Band) because research showed neither group negotiated collectively: every other named band in each area reached its own separate, individually documented outcome, so a shared zone would have misrepresented how these Nations actually engaged with the project. Those individually-documented bands (15 in total, across Treaty 6, Nlaka'pamux/Scw'exmx, and the Secwépemc 2003 claim) are shown as status-colored point markers rather than shapes, since the record supports a specific reserve location and a specific TMX position for each but not a described territorial extent; a marker's color follows its documented status (agreement, opposition, mixed, or no documented position) and is clickable for full sourcing. The marker layer was subsequently widened from those 15 to all 95 First Nations that Canada's own Crown Consultation and Accommodation Report (June 2019) lists as consulted on the project, drawn from CCAR Tables 1 through 4 and cross-referenced against CCAR Table 12, the Crown's list of Indigenous groups holding a commercial agreement on the project. These two classes of marker are deliberately distinguishable and should not be read as equivalent: the original 15 are drawn at full size and carry an individually researched, separately sourced account of that Nation's position, while the 80 added from the CCAR are drawn smaller and state only what the primary documents establish, namely the Nation's presence on the consultation list, its participation in the NEB hearings or the B.C. EAO Section 11 schedules, and whether the Crown records it as holding a commercial agreement. A small marker is therefore a statement about the consultation and benefits record, not about whether that Nation supports or opposes the project; the absence of a written position on those entries reflects the scope of this map, not the absence of a position. Seven consulted Nations (Saddle Lake, Whitefish/Goodfish Lake, Horse Lake, Sucker Creek, Driftpile, Swan River, and Ka:'yu:'k't'h'/Che:k'tles7et'h) sit outside this map's frame and are listed in the legend rather than plotted, since clamping them to the edge would place them somewhere they are not. Métis groups are excluded from this map by choice, not by oversight: CCAR Table 5 lists the Métis Nation of Alberta, its Regional Council Zone 4, Gunn Métis Local Council #55 (Lac Ste. Anne), the Mountain Métis Nation Association, three Métis Settlements, and three B.C. Métis organizations as consulted parties, and Trans Mountain's own Volume 3B states that the Alberta segment crosses "Treaty 6 territory, Treaty 8 territory, and the Métis Nation of Alberta (Region 4)," so a reader should understand the Métis interest in this corridor to be documented and simply out of scope here. Note also that the proponent applied a 100 km buffer when building its Alberta engagement list against 10 km in B.C., and that the pipeline crosses no Indian Reserve anywhere in Alberta while crossing ten in B.C.; the Alberta side of this map is consequently a picture of treaty territory rather than of reserve adjacency, and the two provinces should not be compared as though the same threshold produced both. Bonaparte Indian Band and Splatsin First Nation were researched individually and excluded from the markers: both are confirmed not to be crossed by the pipeline route and had no documented MBA, litigation, or TMX-specific statement. High Bar First Nation, initially left out for lack of data, was added once research confirmed the pipeline passes roughly 50 km from its reserve and that the Nation disputed the government's "negligible impact" finding; its marker's status is red ("opposed/litigated") to reflect that disputed consultation record even though High Bar itself never pursued formal litigation. No portion of this map should be read as an official or legal statement of any Nation's territory. The proposed West Coast Oil Pipeline (Bruderheim to Roberts Bank) is unapproved as of August 2026; its route, terminal design, and Indigenous consultation process are all still pending, so its line and markers should be read as illustrative of the publicly reported corridor, not a finalized alignment.
