Trans Mountain Pipeline (TMX): Route & Broader First Nations Territories

Edmonton, AB → Westridge Marine Terminal, Burnaby, BC · plus the proposed 2026 Bruderheim–Roberts Bank expansion · basemap from real coastline/border/river data, territories schematic

Read this before the map. The coastline, provincial border, rivers, and lakes below are real simplified geometry (Natural Earth 1:50m data), not hand-drawn approximations. The territory areas are still schematic: polygons anchored to landmarks each Nation itself cites (e.g. Howe Sound, Mt Garibaldi, the Athabasca headwaters, the Arrow Lakes), not surveyed or legally-binding boundaries. Many Nations' territories extend further still; where a Nation's own site gives a precise area figure, its polygon roughly targets that scale. Overlap between areas reflects genuinely overlapping/shared or contested claims reported in the sources below, not map error; territories are drawn as dotted outlines rather than filled shapes so that overlapping claims stay individually legible and no Nation's boundary is buried under another's. This is an educational reference assembled from Nations' own public statements, court records, Canada Energy Regulator filings, and news reporting; it is not a legal document and should not be used for consultation, land-claim, or governmental decision-making. Where a boundary leans on general/crowdsourced-style reference rather than a primary document, that is noted in each entry. See Native Land Digital's own caveat that its maps "should not be used for legal claims, land disputes, or governmental decision-making." Methodology

Coastline, the Alberta–BC border, major rivers (Fraser, Athabasca, North Saskatchewan, Columbia) and select lakes are real, simplified boundary/hydrography data from Natural Earth (1:50m cultural & physical vectors), clipped and lightly decimated for this map, not hand-approximated. Pipeline waypoints are drawn from Trans Mountain's 2013 CER/NEB Project Description, CER pipeline-profile and watercourse-crossing records, and corroborating news/encyclopedic sources. Territory polygons are schematic areas anchored to specific landmarks each Nation's own materials, BC Environmental Assessment Office consultation appendices, CER filings, or court records associate with their territory (e.g. Squamish's own site names Howe Sound, Whistler, and Burrard Inlet); landmark coordinates were independently geocoded, not estimated by eye. The full-territory shapes with a cited area figure (SSN ~12,500 km², Squamish ~6,732 km², Tsleil-Waututh ~1,865 km², Musqueam ~5,330 km²) are uniformly scaled about their centroid to match that figure, then rendered as a smoothed curve through the landmark vertices rather than a hard-edged polygon, so the shape reads as an approximate zone rather than a surveyed boundary. That scaling has a trap in it, which SSN's shape fell into: shrinking a described perimeter down to a cited area moves it toward the centroid of the named places, which is only where the claim sits if those places happen to be evenly distributed around it. SSN's 2015 claim is described as centred on the confluence of the North and South Thompson Rivers at Kamloops, but its named perimeter runs much further east than west, so scaling it about its own centroid put the shape roughly 25 km north and 53 km east of the confluence it is defined around. Scaled shapes can now be recentred on a stated centre, and SSN's is; the three corridor bands (Aseniwuche Winewak, Coldwater Indian Band, Lower Nicola Indian Band) are drawn at a fixed width along the relevant valley/corridor and are not area-matched, since they represent a travel corridor rather than a full territorial outline. A caution that applies to all six: the buffer that turns a centreline into a band offsets each vertex along the local normal, so a band whose width approaches or exceeds the length of its own centreline stops reading as a corridor and collapses into a blob centred on its midpoint, and a bend on a segment shorter than the half-width folds the outline back through itself. Widths here are therefore set relative to each centreline's length, not chosen for visual weight. Lower Nicola's band follows the BC EAO's description of its asserted traditional territory as "the valley of the Lower Nicola River": it runs downstream from Merritt along the Nicola, past Shulus and Lower Nicola village, and is drawn at the same soft-edged, schematic width as the other zones rather than around reserve boundaries, since it represents a territory and not a reserve map. It deliberately stops short of Nicola Lake, which is the upper reach and Upper Nicola's end of the valley. Downstream it overlaps ground also asserted by Nooaitch, Shackan and Cook's Ferry, which is a real feature of the Nicola valley rather than a drafting error. Its overlap with Coldwater is confined to Merritt, where the two valleys meet: an earlier version of this map showed the two lying almost on top of one another, which was a drafting error, since Coldwater's band then carried a width of 55 on a centreline about 12 units long and so rendered as a blob over Merritt instead of a valley running south. Both bands were re-anchored to the valley each Nation actually occupies. The reason Lower Nicola warrants a shape at all rather than a point is that three of the ten Indian Reserves the project crosses anywhere in British Columbia are its own (Joeyaska #2, Zoht #4 and Zoht #5), the heaviest reserve exposure of any Nation on the route; those reserves are cited in the entry as evidence for the claim but are not what the outline traces. It deliberately shares Coldwater's colour instead of taking a ninth: both are Scw'exmx bands of the same Nlaka'pamux Nation, so a separate hue would imply a separate Nation, and the map's eight categorical slots are full at a palette separation that will not absorb another. The two are distinguished by a red dotted edge on the Lower Nicola band, by their labels, and by their shapes, so identity never rests on colour alone (the red marks a boundary and carries none of the status meaning red has elsewhere on this map); where they overlap around Merritt the multiply blend darkens, which is the intended signal that two Scw'exmx bands assert adjoining claims there. Two zones were found to overstate their own Nation's stated extent and were corrected against the Nations' own materials. Simpcw was drawn as a corridor band running from Jasper down the North Thompson to Kamloops; Simpcw itself names Vinsulla, about 30 km north of Kamloops, as its southern marker, so the band reached past the territory into Tk'emlúps te Secwépemc's core area around the Thompson confluence, while missing the stated northern, western and southeastern limits entirely. It is now a polygon through the six markers Simpcw publishes: Kakwa Park, Jasper, the Big Bend of the Columbia, northern Adams Lake, Vinsulla and the Goat River. Aseniwuche Winewak was drawn running west past Jasper to Mount Robson in British Columbia; AWN's own materials describe Grande Cache, Jasper and its seven landholdings and name no British Columbia extent, so the band now stops at Jasper. The remaining shapes were checked against their cited sources and left alone. Rather than one blended "Secwépemc territory" shape, this map breaks the Secwépemc-related claims apart by source: Simpcw's own North Thompson valley territory (its own cited hectare figure); Stk'emlupsemc te Secwépemc Nation's (SSN's) own 2015, ~1.25-million-hectare title claim centred on Kamloops (filled); and the separate, broader Dec. 2003 BC Supreme Court joint title claim by Adams Lake, Bonaparte, High Bar, Neskonlith, Simpcw, Spallumcheen, and Whispering Pines (dashed outline, since its landmark description is coarser and was not independently verified beyond four named places). These three overlap in places by design; that is what the source record actually shows; it is not one Secwépemc Nation boundary drawn three times. Every territory on this map is now drawn as a dotted outline in its own colour rather than as a translucent fill. The fills were replaced because they failed precisely where the map is most interesting: a dozen overlapping claims stacked into an indistinct tone exactly over the ground that is most contested, and the blur applied to each edge made every boundary look equally certain whether it came from a Nation's own published area figure or from a coarse description in a consultation appendix. Outlines can overlap indefinitely without any of them being obscured, and a dotted line reads as approximate without needing a blur to say so. Three shapes whose perimeters rest on looser descriptions (Musqueam, Tsawwassen and the 2003 joint claim) carry a longer dash to keep "rough perimeter" visually distinct from "described territory". Because nothing is filled, nothing is permanently labelled on the map either: identity is on demand, and clicking any boundary, pipeline or marker names it in place and opens its full sourced entry in the legend panel. Making that work where claims nest inside one another took some care, since Simpcw's territory and the Stk'emlupsemc te Secwépemc title claim both sit largely within the 2003 joint claim. Clickable areas are stacked smallest-on-top, so a click inside a nested claim selects the nested one rather than the larger shape wrapped around it, and every zone additionally carries an invisible wide band over its own dotted boundary, sitting above all the interiors, so the enclosing claim can always be selected by clicking its line even where a smaller claim covers its middle. Place names and their dots are not clickable, so they cannot intercept a click meant for the territory underneath. The result was checked by scanning a grid across the whole map and confirming that all twelve zones are reachable, rather than by clicking a few and assuming. Two colour choices are worth stating. Lower Nicola is drawn in red at the reader's request to separate it from Coldwater, which is the nearest competing boundary; red carries none of the status meaning it has on the markers. And the 2003 joint claim was moved off dark green to teal, because it had been near-identical to the Stk'emlupsemc te Secwépemc claim it overlaps around Kamloops, which is the one pair on this map a reader most needs to tell apart. The Stó:lō zone stays a single blended shape deliberately: unlike the other groupings on this map, the Stó:lō Collective genuinely did negotiate TMX consultation as one body, so blending them is the accurate choice, not a simplification. Its outline is no longer schematic. It was a corridor band drawn along the pipeline from Yale to the Pitt River, which described the route rather than the territory, and is now traced from the Stó:lō Service Agency's own published S'ólh Téméxw map. That map is a satellite image rather than a data file, so it was georeferenced against three fixed features on it: the 49th parallel, which its southern boundary follows exactly and which appears as a dead-straight line; its own 1:250,000 scale bar; and the Tsawwassen ferry causeway. The transform was then checked against a feature not used to build it, the Fraser Canyon at Hope, which lands at longitude -121.44 against Hope's true -121.44. The boundary is sampled to 21 vertices and smoothed, so it is a faithful outline of the published line and not a reproduction of it, and the source map clips its own boundary at the frame in the southeast, so the territory may reach a little further east than drawn. The "Nlaka'pamux / Scw'exmx Tribal Council" zone was narrowed to a single flagship band (Coldwater Indian Band) because research showed the group did not negotiate collectively: every other named band reached its own separate, individually documented outcome, so a shared zone would have misrepresented how these Nations actually engaged with the project. The Edmonton-area zone went through the same correction and then a further one. It began as "Treaty 6 Nations", was narrowed to Enoch Cree Nation for the same reason, and is now the Treaty 6 area itself. That is the accurate role for a shape here: Treaty 6 is a real, dated, bounded instrument covering the whole Alberta run of the pipeline, whereas "Treaty 6 Nations" was never a negotiating body for this project. Enoch, Paul, Alexander and Alexis Nakota Sioux each keep their own marker inside it, since each reached its own outcome. Those individually-documented bands (15 in total, across Treaty 6, Nlaka'pamux/Scw'exmx, and the Secwépemc 2003 claim) are shown as status-colored point markers rather than shapes, since the record supports a specific reserve location and a specific TMX position for each but not a described territorial extent; a marker's color follows its documented status (agreement, opposition, mixed, or no documented position) and is clickable for full sourcing. The marker layer was subsequently widened from those 15 to all 95 First Nations that Canada's own Crown Consultation and Accommodation Report (June 2019) lists as consulted on the project, drawn from CCAR Tables 1 through 4 and cross-referenced against CCAR Table 12, the Crown's list of Indigenous groups holding a commercial agreement on the project. These two classes of marker are deliberately distinguishable and should not be read as equivalent: the original 15 are drawn at full size and carry an individually researched, separately sourced account of that Nation's position, while the 80 added from the CCAR are drawn smaller and state only what the primary documents establish, namely the Nation's presence on the consultation list, its participation in the NEB hearings or the B.C. EAO Section 11 schedules, and whether the Crown records it as holding a commercial agreement. A small marker is therefore a statement about the consultation and benefits record, not about whether that Nation supports or opposes the project; the absence of a written position on those entries reflects the scope of this map, not the absence of a position. The basemap was later rebuilt from Natural Earth 1:10m data over a wider box, lon -129 to -105.5 and lat 45.8 to 57.5, replacing an earlier 1:50m clip that carried only British Columbia and Alberta and stopped at lon -112.5. Two things follow from that. Seven consulted Nations (Saddle Lake, Whitefish/Goodfish Lake, Horse Lake, Sucker Creek, Driftpile, Swan River, and Ka:'yu:'k't'h'/Che:k'tles7et'h) previously fell outside the frame and had to be listed in the legend rather than plotted; they now sit inside it and appear as ordinary markers. And the Treaty 6 outline, which used to run off the eastern edge, now closes on screen. The basemap is embedded in the page rather than drawn from a tile service, and not by preference: this map runs under a content policy that blocks external requests, so a Google or OpenStreetMap tile layer would render as blank space. What detail there is comes from vector data compiled into the file, which is why the map shows coastline, provincial and state boundaries, lakes and rivers but no roads, terrain shading or place-level imagery. Métis groups are excluded from this map by choice, not by oversight: CCAR Table 5 lists the Métis Nation of Alberta, its Regional Council Zone 4, Gunn Métis Local Council #55 (Lac Ste. Anne), the Mountain Métis Nation Association, three Métis Settlements, and three B.C. Métis organizations as consulted parties, and Trans Mountain's own Volume 3B states that the Alberta segment crosses "Treaty 6 territory, Treaty 8 territory, and the Métis Nation of Alberta (Region 4)," so a reader should understand the Métis interest in this corridor to be documented and simply out of scope here. Note also that the proponent applied a 100 km buffer when building its Alberta engagement list against 10 km in B.C., and that the pipeline crosses no Indian Reserve anywhere in Alberta while crossing ten in B.C.; the Alberta side of this map is consequently a picture of treaty territory rather than of reserve adjacency, and the two provinces should not be compared as though the same threshold produced both. Bonaparte Indian Band and Splatsin First Nation were researched individually and excluded from the markers: both are confirmed not to be crossed by the pipeline route and had no documented MBA, litigation, or TMX-specific statement. High Bar First Nation, initially left out for lack of data, was added once research confirmed the pipeline passes roughly 50 km from its reserve and that the Nation disputed the government's "negligible impact" finding; its marker's status is red ("opposed/litigated") to reflect that disputed consultation record even though High Bar itself never pursued formal litigation. Labels, markers and line weights do not scale one-for-one with the map when zooming: they grow as roughly the cube root of the zoom factor, so magnifying the view spreads the geography apart faster than the type grows. Zooming in therefore separates crowded labels and enlarges them at the same time, rather than trading one against the other. Place-name sizes at the default view are set to the largest value that still leaves the label layout free of overlaps, which was verified by measuring every rendered label box rather than by eye. No portion of this map should be read as an official or legal statement of any Nation's territory. The proposed West Coast Oil Pipeline (Bruderheim to Roberts Bank) is unapproved as of August 2026; its route, terminal design, and Indigenous consultation process are all still pending, so its line and markers should be read as illustrative of the publicly reported corridor, not a finalized alignment. Alberta's own submission says the exact route within the corridor has not been finalized and that alternative options remain under review. Two further simplifications are worth stating plainly. First, this map draws one line between Bruderheim and Hope, but the Original and Optimized Corridors are not identical over that distance: Alberta's appendix map shows them parting company around Edmonton, where the Optimized line passes north of the city and the Original dips through it, and again between Blue River and Clearwater. The northern option is the one drawn here. Second, the shared section is rendered directly from this map's existing Trans Mountain route geometry rather than as an independent set of waypoints, which is faithful to the submission's own description but means the proposed line cannot, by construction, show any place where the new corridor would depart from the old one between Spruce Grove and Hope.
